Plain-language status report                                9 November 2026

WHAT THIS DOCUMENT IS

A non-technical companion to backend/docs/TRUSTSCORE_DATA_SOURCES.md. It
answers three questions: what the TrustScore can tell a broker today, what it
cannot yet see, and which of those gaps need a decision from the business
rather than more engineering.

Every number here was read from the live database on 9 November 2026.


================================================================================
1.  THE SHORT VERSION
================================================================================

The scoring algorithm is complete and running. All 1,839 operators have a
score. Six public data sources feed it, and four of the six risk categories a
broker sees are now backed by real evidence.

Two categories cannot be fully assessed, and in both cases the obstacle is
access to an FAA system rather than unfinished work on our side. We have
confirmed this from two independent networks, so it is not a configuration
problem we can engineer around.

One further input — the age of an operator's charter certificate — is an
acknowledged estimate. Both routes to a real figure have been tested and
neither works.

Nothing in the system currently overstates an operator. Where we cannot see
something, the report says so.

3.  ONE THING WORTH KNOWING: TRADING NAMES
================================================================================

An operator that rebrands used to lose its entire history. Accident and
enforcement records are filed under whatever name the company used at the
time, so searching only the current name returned nothing and the operator
scored as though it had a clean record.

We now resolve every operator to all 1,914 names its certificate has traded
under, in both directions. Searching finds the history regardless of renaming.

This was not a theoretical risk. One operator scored 98.93 out of 100 — the
top tier — purely because nothing matched its current name.


================================================================================
4.  WHAT THE SCORE CANNOT YET SEE
================================================================================

4.1  AIRCRAFT OWNERSHIP AND TITLE  —  needs FAA access

  A broker wants to know whether an aircraft has clean title: no outstanding
  loans, no mechanic's liens, no history of being shuffled between shell
  companies.

  We can see part of this. The FAA publishes a rolling index of documents
  filed in roughly the last 180 days, and we read it — 99 active loans or
  liens across 175 aircraft.

  What we cannot see is anything older. A lien recorded three years ago and
  never released simply does not appear in that index. So finding nothing
  proves nothing, and the report correctly shows this category as
  "not assessed" rather than "clear".

  The permanent record exists, in an FAA system called CARES. It holds every
  bill of sale, loan and lien release for an aircraft since it was built.

  WE CANNOT REACH IT. The system refuses our requests, from the production
  server and from a developer machine on a different network. This is not a
  firewall or routing issue we can work around — the FAA appears to require
  registered access.

  >> DECISION NEEDED: obtain FAA registered access to CARES, or accept that
     title verification stays incomplete. This is a procurement question, not
     an engineering one.


4.2  FLEET STABILITY  —  needs time, and the weekly job to keep running

  Rapid fleet turnover is a recognised warning sign: an operator shedding or
  absorbing aircraft quickly may be in trouble. Measuring it means comparing
  today's fleet against the same fleet twelve months ago.

  The measurement is built and working. What we lack is history. We began
  recording fleet composition recently and currently hold 31 days.

  We cannot backfill this. The FAA overwrites its weekly file, and the
  Internet Archive — which we checked, expecting a few years of snapshots —
  holds exactly ONE historical copy. So the only way to get this data is to
  keep collecting it.

  Usable signal: around MAY 2027.
  Full twelve-month measure: around NOVEMBER 2027.

  >> ACTION: a weekly automated job must run without interruption. Every week
     missed pushes those dates back, and the data cannot be recovered later.


4.3  CERTIFICATE AGE  —  an acknowledged estimate

  How long an operator has held its charter certificate affects its score: a
  thirty-year-old operation is treated differently from a six-month-old one.

  We do not have this date. The FAA's published roster does not include it.

  Two substitutes were tested and both failed:

    - Business registration date. Available for about half of operators, and
      it measures something different — when the company was formed, not when
      it was certified to fly charters.

    - Aircraft registration dates from the FAA registry. Looked promising at
      98% coverage, but it agreed with the other source only 16% of the time,
      with disagreements running equally in both directions. The reason is
      structural: that date resets whenever an aircraft is sold, so it
      measures recent purchases, not operator history.

  The one system that holds the real date, FAA AVInfo, is unreachable for the
  same reason as CARES above.

  Current behaviour: where the date is unknown we assume ten years and FLAG
  THE SCORE AS BASED ON AN ESTIMATE. The assumption is close to the real
  median we measured (10.7 years), so it is reasonable on average — but it is
  not evidence for any individual operator.

  >> DECISION NEEDED: request certificate issue dates directly from the FAA
     office that publishes the roster, or accept the flagged estimate. We
     recommend accepting it; the uncertainty is disclosed, not hidden.


4.4  TWO SMALLER GAPS

  Minor safety occurrences. Runway excursions, gear problems and bird strikes
  that never reach a formal accident report are not currently collected. A
  public database exists; this is straightforward work, not blocked.

  Industry watchdog reports. Commercial services publish operator complaint
  and dispute data that would strengthen the commercial-integrity assessment.

  >> DECISION NEEDED: whether to license watchdog data. This is a cost and
     terms-of-service question.


================================================================================
5.  HOW WE HANDLE WHAT WE CANNOT SEE
================================================================================

Each operator report carries six categories, colour-coded:

  GREEN    checked, nothing adverse found
  YELLOW   something found, but not severe or not recent
  RED      serious and current
  GREY     NOT ASSESSED — we could not check this properly

The grey state matters more than it sounds. Without it, "we found nothing"
and "we could not look" produce the same clean-looking report, and the second
is misleading. Any category resting on a source we could not query is marked
grey rather than green.

Two deliberate consequences:

  - Aircraft title stays grey even when we DO find liens, because the index
    we read cannot prove the absence of older ones.

  - Fleet stability stays grey until enough history accumulates.

We also corrected a related problem. An operator with a 1988 accident was
showing RED on flight safety — reading to a broker as a current hazard —
while the score itself had correctly discounted a 38-year-old event to
almost nothing. Flags now age at the same rate as the score. The accident is
still disclosed in the written report; it is no longer presented as current.

================================================================================
7.  THE HONEST SUMMARY
================================================================================

The TrustScore is built on six live public data sources and produces a
defensible score for every operator in the database. Four of six risk
categories are evidenced.

The two that are not are limited by access to FAA systems, which we have
verified from two networks. We have not worked around this and do not believe
it can be worked around with development effort.

Where the system cannot see something it says so, rather than implying a
clean result. That is the single most important property to preserve: a score
that overstates an operator is worse than one that admits uncertainty.
