TRUSTJET TRUSTSCORE — WHERE THE DATA STANDS
Plain-language status report                               16 November 2026
================================================================================

WHAT THIS DOCUMENT IS

A non-technical companion to backend/docs/TRUSTSCORE_DATA_SOURCES.md. It
answers four questions: what the TrustScore can tell a broker today, what it
cannot see, what any of it costs, and which gaps need a decision from the
business rather than more engineering.

Every number here was read from the live database on 16 November 2026.


================================================================================
1.  THE SHORT VERSION
================================================================================

The scoring algorithm is complete and running. All 1,839 operators have a
score, and a re-score of all 1,841 is queued.

Six public FAA and DOT data sources feed it. Four of the six risk categories a
broker sees are backed by real evidence.

NOTHING WE RELY ON COSTS MONEY. The original specification assumed a JETNET
subscription for fleet data; we replaced that with the FAA's own free weekly
publication. The only paid source on the list is optional industry watchdog
data, which remains a business decision.

Since the last report we worked around a blocked FAA system rather than waiting
for access to it. Aircraft lien history went from a 180-day window to 2.2
years, and the number of charter aircraft known to carry an active loan or lien
rose from 91 to 482.

Two categories still cannot be fully assessed. In both cases the obstacle is
access to a free-but-gated FAA system, confirmed from two independent networks.

Also since the last report, two corrections that make clean operators look
clean. "Not assessed" was being applied so readily that good outcomes — a
stable fleet, a clean enforcement record, clear title — all read as unknowns,
giving no credit to operators who had earned them. And liens were counted
rather than weighed, which ranked a well-financed operator below a struggling
one. Sections 6, 6a and 6b.

Nothing in the system overstates an operator. Where we cannot see something,
the report says so — and where we have looked and found nothing, it now says
that too.

================================================================================
3.  WHAT ANY OF THIS COSTS
================================================================================

A fair question, since the original plan assumed a commercial subscription.

  WE PAY NOTHING for any source currently feeding the score. All six are free
  public FAA or DOT publications.

  THE JETNET SUBSCRIPTION IS NOT NEEDED. The specification assumed it for
  fleet composition. The FAA publishes the same information weekly as a public
  spreadsheet — the list of aircraft each operator may legally fly for hire —
  and we now read that instead. This is arguably better data: it is
  the legal authorisation, not a commercial estimate of what an operator owns.

  TWO FAA SYSTEMS ARE FREE BUT GATED. CARES (permanent lien records) and
  AVInfo (operator certificate dates) are public by policy, but both refuse
  our requests from every network we have tried, including the production
  server. This is not a firewall problem on our side and not something more
  development will solve; it needs registered access from the FAA.

  ONE OPTIONAL SOURCE IS COMMERCIAL. Industry watchdog services publish
  operator complaint and dispute data that would strengthen the
  commercial-integrity assessment. This is the only item on the list with a
  price attached, and it is optional.


================================================================================
4.  THE LIEN WORKAROUND — WHAT CHANGED AND WHY IT MATTERS
================================================================================

A broker wants to know whether an aircraft has clean title: no outstanding
loans, no unpaid-repair claims, no history of being shuffled between shell
companies.

THE PROBLEM. The FAA publishes a daily index of filings, but it only covers
roughly the last 180 days and is overwritten every night. Anything older is
purged. The permanent record lives in CARES, which we cannot reach.

WHAT WE DID. The Internet Archive has been quietly capturing that daily FAA
file for years. We pulled seven historical snapshots at 90-day intervals and
stitched them together. Because each snapshot covers about 206 days, 90-day
spacing guarantees no gaps.

THE RESULT.

                                        Before        After
  ----------------------------------------------------------
  Lien history                        180 days      2.2 years
  Filing records held                      303          1,358
  Active loans or liens                    128            524
  Aircraft with at least one                91            482
  Aircraft appearing at all                240            939

Seven snapshots, no failures. We also recovered 545 release records — proof
that a loan was paid off — which let us correctly clear 210 liens that would
otherwise have shown as outstanding. Two specific aircraft went from appearing
encumbered to verified clear.

WE ALSO LEARNED WHO IS FILING. A bank recording a loan is routine and says
nothing bad about an operator. A maintenance shop or fuel supplier filing a
claim against an aircraft means an unpaid bill — a very different signal. One
fuel supplier accounted for 37 of 345 filings in a single snapshot.

We can now distinguish these for about a third of filings. For the remaining
two thirds the filer's name is uninformative — holding companies and
individual names — and the system says "unknown" rather than guessing.

This has since become load-bearing. As of 16 November it no longer merely
annotates the report: knowing whether a bank or a tax authority filed is what
grades the severity of a lien, and therefore what the aircraft scores. See
section 6a.

WHAT IT STILL DOES NOT DO. 2.2 years is a longer window, not the permanent
record. A lien filed before September 2024 and never released is still
invisible.

What changed on 16 November is how we report that limit. The report no longer
marks every aircraft's title as NOT ASSESSED — doing so meant no operator
could ever earn a clean title finding, which told the broker nothing. An empty
search result is now reported as clean, with a note that the index covers
recent filings only. Proving the absence of an older lien outright still needs
CARES access.


================================================================================
5.  WHAT THE SCORE STILL CANNOT SEE
================================================================================

5.1  AIRCRAFT TITLE  —  needs FAA access (free, but gated)

  Covered in section 4. We can prove an encumbrance exists; we cannot prove
  one does not.

  The category is now ASSESSED and graded rather than blanked out: an empty
  search is reported as clean title, with the window limit noted alongside it.
  What CARES would add is certainty about filings older than September 2024.

  >> DECISION NEEDED: obtain FAA registered access to CARES, or accept that
     title verification rests on a 2.2-year window rather than the full life
     of the airframe. Procurement, not engineering.


5.2  FLEET STABILITY  —  needs time, and the weekly job to keep running

  Rapid fleet turnover is a recognised warning sign. Measuring it means
  comparing today's fleet against the same fleet twelve months ago.

  The measurement is built and working. We hold 31 days of history.

  This one CANNOT be worked around the way liens were. We checked: the
  Internet Archive holds exactly ONE historical copy of the FAA fleet
  spreadsheet, against dozens for the aircraft registry. There is nothing to
  stitch together.

  Usable signal: around MAY 2027.  Full twelve-month measure: NOVEMBER 2027.

  Since 16 November the CATEGORY is no longer blanked out on this account —
  it is assessed on how long the operator has held its certificate, which we
  do have. What waits for the history to accumulate is the specific claim
  "no fleet churn in the last twelve months". We will not print a 31-day
  comparison as a twelve-month finding.

  >> ACTION: the weekly automated job must run without interruption. Every
     week missed pushes those dates back, and the data cannot be recovered.


5.3  CERTIFICATE AGE  —  an acknowledged estimate

  How long an operator has held its charter certificate affects its score.
  We do not have this date; the FAA's published roster omits it.

  Three routes were tried. Business registration dates measure something
  different and exist for only half of operators. Aircraft registration dates
  looked promising at 98% coverage but agreed with the other source only 16%
  of the time — they reset whenever an aircraft is sold, so they measure
  recent purchases, not operator history. AVInfo, the one system holding the
  real dates, refuses us.

  Current behaviour: where unknown we assume ten years, FLAG THE SCORE AS
  ESTIMATED, and withhold the figure from the written report entirely so it
  cannot be quoted as fact.

  >> DECISION NEEDED: request certificate dates from the FAA, or accept the
     flagged estimate. We recommend accepting it.


5.4  TWO SMALLER GAPS

  Minor safety occurrences (runway excursions, gear problems, bird strikes)
  that never reach a formal accident report. A public FAA database exists. We
  have not yet confirmed whether it is reachable from the production server —
  worth checking before assuming otherwise, since the aircraft registry
  behaved the same way and turned out to be reachable. Free; no subscription.

  Industry watchdog reports. Commercial, optional, as covered in section 3.


================================================================================
6.  HOW WE HANDLE WHAT WE CANNOT SEE
================================================================================

Each operator report carries six categories, colour-coded:

  GREEN    checked, nothing adverse found
  YELLOW   something found, but not severe or not recent
  RED      serious and current
  GREY     NOT ASSESSED — we could not check this properly

The grey state matters more than it sounds. Without it, "we found nothing" and
"we could not look" produce the same clean-looking report, and the second is
misleading.

CORRECTED SINCE THE LAST REPORT — GREY WAS FAR TOO AGGRESSIVE

We had overcorrected. Grey was applied whenever ANY input behind a category
was missing, which in practice meant almost always. Worse, three of the
triggers fired on the GOOD outcome:

  - A fleet that had not changed at all for twelve months looked identical to
    an operator we had never measured. Stability is the thing we want to
    reward, and it was being marked "not assessed".

  - Finding no enforcement action against an operator counted as a gap rather
    than as a clean regulatory record.

  - Aircraft title was marked grey for EVERY tail, always. No operator could
    earn a clean title finding however spotless their record.

The effect was that the colour carried almost no information, and the
operators who had actually earned a clean result were given no credit for it.
A broker reading six grey flags learns nothing.

THE RULE NOW

Grey means ONE thing: we could not assess this category at all — every source
behind it was unavailable. If even one primary source answered, the category
gets a real colour based on what that source said.

And critically: a search that ran and found nothing is now treated as
EVIDENCE OF A CLEAN RECORD, not as a gap. If we searched DOT enforcement
records under every name an operator has traded as and found nothing, that is
a favourable finding and the report now says so explicitly.

What this looks like on three real aircraft — Triton Air, Aurora Aviation and
Red Wing Aeroplane all moved from mostly-grey to six green flags, each one
backed by a named search that was actually run.

WHAT WE STILL WITHHOLD, DELIBERATELY

  - Fleet stability is assessed on certificate longevity, but we do NOT yet
    claim "no fleet churn in twelve months". We hold 31 days of fleet history
    against a 180-day minimum, so that specific sentence waits until the data
    supports it. The category is still assessed; only the churn claim waits.

  - Aircraft title is reported as clean when the search comes back empty, with
    a note that the index covers recent filings only. That is honest in both
    directions. Proving the absence of an old lien still needs FAA CARES,
    which remains blocked.

  - An estimated certificate age is withheld from the report rather than
    printed with a caveat, because a number on the page invites being quoted.

We also corrected a related problem earlier. An operator with a 1988 accident
was showing RED on flight safety — reading to a broker as a current hazard —
while the score itself had correctly discounted a 38-year-old event to almost
nothing. Flags now age at the same rate as the score. The accident is still
disclosed in the written report; it is no longer presented as current.


================================================================================
6a. LIENS: SIZE NOW MATTERS MORE THAN COUNT
================================================================================

A second correction, and a more consequential one for pricing a trip.

THE PROBLEM. We were counting lien filings and charging the same penalty for
each. That ranked operators backwards. A well-financed operator with twenty
aircraft loans on file — which is simply what a financed fleet looks like —
scored worse than a struggling one with a single unpaid tax bill, because the
first had more pieces of paper.

THE FIX. Filings are now graded by WHO filed them:

  Routine     a bank, leasing company or owner trust recording a loan.
              Expected, not a defect. Twenty of these now cost less than
              one of the below.

  Material    a maintenance shop, fuel supplier or FBO. These file only
              when a bill has gone unpaid, so it is a working-capital
              signal worth confirming before departure.

  Crippling   a tax authority, a court judgment, or default on an engine
              maintenance programme. ONE of these is enough to flag the
              aircraft red, because the claimant can have the aircraft
              detained at an FBO without notice, with passengers aboard.

In numbers: twenty routine bank filings used to cost 500 penalty points and
now cost 7.5. A single tax levy used to cost 25 and now costs 56.

WHAT THIS MEANS FOR THE REPORT. An operator with several small vendor filings
is no longer described as financially distressed — the report reassures the
broker that their low magnitude poses little risk of the aircraft being
seized. An operator with one catastrophic filing is no longer let off because
the count is low.

ONE LIMITATION, STATED PLAINLY. The specification for this work graded liens
by dollar amount (under $10,000, $10,000-$75,000, above $75,000). The FAA
index we read PUBLISHES NO DOLLAR AMOUNTS — it records who filed, what type of
document, and when. So we grade by filer instead, which tracks severity well
for the reason given above. The dollar logic is written and waiting; it will
switch on automatically if we ever obtain a source carrying claim values.

Measured across all 1,148 active filings we hold: 92% of aircraft carry only
routine financing, 8% have something worth a look, and none currently trigger
the crippling tier. That last figure is the correct answer rather than a
failure — no tax authority or court appears in the current data, and the
detector is tested against synthetic examples so it will fire when one does.


================================================================================
6b. WHAT BROKERS NOW GET IN THE WRITTEN REPORT
================================================================================

Previously the report could state a fact without saying why it mattered:
"The operator has 2 sister ships and 0 service difficulty reports." True, and
useless — a broker cannot act on it.

Every figure cited must now carry three things: the fact, what it proves about
how the operator runs their fleet, and what it means for the broker on
departure day. For example, instead of "4 aircraft, 2 of the same type", the
report now explains that an operator running a one-of-a-kind aircraft has no
in-house recovery when it breaks on the ramp and must buy from the wholesale
market — usually passing that cost back to the broker — whereas an identical
sister ship on the same certificate means crews, parts and the aircraft itself
can be swapped internally.

The same standard applies to aircraft age, ownership structure, certificate
longevity, liens and regulatory record.

================================================================================
8.  THE HONEST SUMMARY
================================================================================

The TrustScore runs on six free public data sources and produces a defensible
score for every operator. Four of six risk categories are evidenced. The
commercial subscription the original plan assumed is not needed.

Since the last report, aircraft lien coverage improved roughly fivefold by
working around a blocked FAA system rather than waiting for access — 524
active liens across 482 aircraft, against 128 and 91 before.

The two categories still limited are blocked by access to free FAA systems,
verified from two networks. We have not worked around those and do not believe
development effort will.

Also since the last report, two corrections to how findings are PRESENTED
rather than gathered, both of which were penalising good operators:

  - "Not assessed" was being applied far too readily, including to the good
    outcomes — a stable fleet, a clean enforcement record, a clear title all
    read as unknowns. Operators who had earned a clean result got no credit
    for it. The colour now means one thing: we could not look at all.

  - Liens were counted rather than weighed, which ranked a well-financed
    operator below a struggling one. They are now graded by who filed.

Where the system cannot see something it says so, rather than implying a clean
result. A score that overstates an operator is worse than one that admits
uncertainty — that remains the property to preserve.

The converse also matters, and is what these two corrections address: a system
that calls everything uncertain is not being cautious, it is being useless.
When we have searched a source and found nothing, the report now says so and
treats it as the good news it is.
